Design for Assembly (DFA/DFM)

Step 01

Before any tray or shelf went to tooling, every part was reviewed against DFA/DFM guidelines — part count, fastening method, and assembly sequence — so the organizer could be built on the line with basic hand tools and no special jigs. Locating tabs, self-piloting holes, and snap-formed edges were added to the sheet metal parts so components could only assemble one, correct way, cutting mis-assembly and assembly time on the shop floor.

Shelf Load Testing — Round 1 & 2

Step 02

The shelf brackets were validated in two rounds of load testing. The first round loaded a wall-mounted bracket with stacked plate weights to find the failure point and check bracket deflection at the joint. The second round moved to a graduated scale board so deflection could be measured directly against a mix of everyday loads, confirming the bracket held its rated capacity with margin before sign-off.

Prototype Iteration — Cover & Pocket

Step 03

The document pocket and its cover went through an extra iteration once the first sheet metal sample was in hand. The single-pocket production sample was reworked into a compartmentalized, multi-slot cover so documents and small valuables could be separated instead of sitting loose in one bay — improving usability without adding to the footprint the pocket already occupied.

Documentation & Commercialization Step 04

eBOM, mBOM, Costing & Procurement

Once the design was validated through DFA review and load testing, I took ownership of the commercial side of the handoff as well — structuring the engineering and manufacturing bills of material, working the part-level costing, and coordinating with procurement so the design didn't just sit as a released drawing but was actually ready to be sourced, quoted, and built.

01
eBOM
Built the engineering BOM directly from the CAD assembly — every tray, divider, bracket, and fastener structured with part numbers and revision levels traceable back to the released drawings.
02
mBOM
Converted the eBOM into a manufacturing BOM — sequencing sub-assemblies, adding process-consumables (fasteners, coating, packing), and aligning it to how the line would actually build the organizer.
03
Costing
Worked out part-level costing — raw material and sheet utilization, forming and coating operations, and fastener/hardware cost — rolled up into a should-cost for the finished organizer set.
04
Procurement Coordination
Shared drawings and the mBOM with vendors for sheet metal and coating quotes, reviewed vendor feedback on formability and tooling, and closed open queries so sourcing could move ahead without redesign loops.
The Challenge

More usable storage inside a volume that can't grow

A safe's external dimensions are fixed by the security rating and door mechanism, so any improvement to how customers actually use the interior has to come from smarter organization — trays, tiered shelving, and document dividers — rather than from more space.

Approach

Iterating from proof of concept to a producible, costed design

I worked through several internal layout concepts against real customer use cases — cash, jewelry, documents — before settling on a configuration and pushing it through prototype validation, testing, and cost and procurement review.

  • Modeled multiple interior layout concepts in UG NX and Fusion 360 and benchmarked usable volume against the fixed safe envelope.
  • Built proof-of-concept prototypes to test tray strength, drawer travel, and how documents and valuables actually sat inside each configuration.
  • Reviewed every sheet metal part for DFA/DFM before tooling — part count, fastening, and one-way assembly features.
  • Ran two rounds of shelf load testing to validate bracket capacity and deflection against rated loads.
  • Iterated the document pocket cover from a single open bay to a multi-slot design after the first production sample.
  • Selected sheet metal and coating specs for the organizer components based on cost and manufacturing lead time.
  • Structured the eBOM from the released CAD assembly and converted it into a sequenced mBOM for the shop floor.
  • Built part-level should-cost estimates covering material, forming, coating, and hardware.
  • Coordinated with procurement and vendors on quotes, formability feedback, and tooling queries to keep sourcing on schedule.
Outcome

A usable-space improvement, ready to source and build

The finalized organizer layout made significantly better use of the fixed interior volume, giving customers clearer, more accessible storage for documents and valuables without any change to the safe's external footprint. With the eBOM, mBOM, costing, and procurement coordination worked out alongside the drawings, the design moved straight into sourcing and production planning without rework loops.

Key Takeaway

"When the outer envelope is fixed, the design work moves entirely inside — every millimeter of layout has to earn its place, and every part still has to be costed and sourceable."
